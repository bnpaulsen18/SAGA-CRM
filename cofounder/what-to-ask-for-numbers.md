# What to ask for — specific numbers

> Concrete asks for a Sync(d) agreement. Keep out of git.
> These are business-standard structures, not legal advice — an attorney papers the final deal. But you should walk in already knowing your numbers.

---

## 0. Four questions you must answer before naming any number

You cannot price your cut until you know what you're taking a cut *of*. Ask Sync(d) these directly — they're reasonable questions and their willingness to answer tells you a lot:

1. **What does a typical Forge customer actually pay, broken into: assessment, implementation, managed services, and any software/licence line?** This is the number everything below depends on.
2. **Is there a per-seat or per-month price for a Forge product, or is it all wrapped into services?** (Your website has no pricing, which suggests the latter.)
3. **How many nonprofit prospects are in your pipeline today — real ones, with names?**
4. **How many people are at Sync(d), and is anyone else doing anything in nonprofits?**

Until you have #1 and #2, any percentage you agree to is a guess. If they won't share, that itself is information.

---

## 1. The single most valuable financial decision: reprice for the channel

**$100/month is the wrong price for the Microsoft route.**

That price was set for a three-person nonprofit signing up on a website by themselves. A nonprofit that runs Dynamics, has a Microsoft admin, and hires an implementation partner is a **completely different customer** — bigger, better funded, and already spending five or six figures on the engagement.

What comparable products charge:

| Product | Price |
|---|---|
| Bloomerang / Kindful / Neon (small-org donor CRMs) | $99–200/mo |
| Blackbaud Raiser's Edge NXT (mid/large nonprofits) | ~$450–1,500+/mo |
| A Dynamics implementation for a mid-size nonprofit | $25k–150k one-time, plus managed services |

**Ask for a channel price of $400–800/month per nonprofit**, tiered by donor-record count or staff seats. That is still cheap next to Raiser's Edge, trivial next to the implementation cost, and it makes every downstream percentage worth 4–8× more.

Do this *before* you agree to any revenue share. A 25% share of $800 beats a 50% share of $100.

> Keep $100/mo for the self-serve product at sagacrm.io if you keep it running. Two prices for two channels is normal and expected.

## 2. Scenario A — SAGA stays yours, Sync(d) sells it

This is the structure I'd push for. You own the AppSource listing; they're a sales channel.

### Your asks

| Term | Open at | Settle at | Walk below |
|---|---|---|---|
| **List price to nonprofit** | $800/mo | $500/mo | $300/mo |
| **Reseller discount to Sync(d)** | 20% (you net $640) | 30% (you net $350–560) | 40% (you net $180–300) |
| **Or: referral fee** if they only introduce | You pay 10% of year 1 | 15% | 20% |
| **Exclusivity** | None | Dynamics customers only, 18 months, expires unless they hit targets | Never open-ended, never all channels |
| **Annual minimum** to keep exclusivity | 25 customers/yr | 12 customers/yr | 6 customers/yr |
| **Implementation pull-through fee** | 10% of engagements SAGA wins | 5% | 0% (acceptable — this is a stretch ask) |
| **Term** | 2 years, auto-renew | 2 years | Nothing over 3 years without a break clause |

**What this earns you.** At a $500/mo list, 30% reseller discount, 12 nonprofits in year one: **$50,400/year recurring**, growing with each customer, and you still own the product outright.

**On the pull-through fee** — asking for a slice of *their* services revenue when they're already giving you a reseller margin is aggressive, and they may well say no. Ask anyway. If SAGA is what wins a $60k implementation, 5% is $3,000 and it costs them nothing they wouldn't have otherwise. Trade it away for something else if they push back.

### Non-negotiable in this scenario

- **The Partner Center publisher account is in your name.** Everything else is worthless without it.
- **You keep the SAGA name, domain and IP.** Licensed to them to resell, never assigned.
- **You're named as technical lead on nonprofit engagements.**

## 3. Scenario B — SAGA becomes a Sync(d) product

Here you're contributing the product, so you're being paid for an asset *and* for ongoing work. Ask for both. Don't let one absorb the other.

### The package, in four parts

**Part 1 — Base compensation**
A salary increase reflecting a promotion to practice lead, not a thank-you.
**Ask: +$20,000–40,000** on current base, or a market rate for "Practice Lead" if that's higher.

**Part 2 — Commission on nonprofit revenue**
This is the part most worth fighting for, because it attaches to the *engagement*, not to a module price.

| | Number |
|---|---|
| Open at | **10%** of total nonprofit engagement revenue (assessment + implementation + managed services + software) |
| Settle at | **5–7%** |
| Walk below | **3%** |

Sales reps typically get 8–12% of first-year value on new logos. You're bringing the IP *and* doing the technical delivery, so opening at 10% is defensible, not greedy.

> On a $60k first-year nonprofit engagement, 7% is **$4,200 per customer**. Ten customers is $42,000/year.

**Part 3 — Payment for the IP itself**
Separate from salary and commission. Two ways to structure it, and I'd take the second:

- **Lump sum:** $50,000–150,000 for the IP, brand and design. Open at $150k. Be aware the public MIT licence weakens the legal argument for a big number — what you're really selling is the brand, the domain, the design work, and the fact that you won't go build it for someone else.
- **Earnout (better):** a smaller upfront ($25–50k) plus **$100–200 per nonprofit customer per month for 3–5 years**. If it works, this pays far more than a lump sum. If it doesn't, you lost less than you would have by holding out for cash.

**Part 4 — Equity**
For contributing a product to a small services company:

| Sync(d) size | Realistic ask |
|---|---|
| Under ~15 people | **2–5%** |
| 15–50 people | **1–3%** |
| 50+ | **0.5–1.5%**, or skip equity and take more commission |

Ask for **4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff** — standard, and asking for standard terms signals you know what you're doing.

If they say equity is off the table (common at services firms — they often don't want outside shareholders), **convert it into a longer revenue-share tail**: e.g. 5% of nonprofit revenue for 5 years, surviving your departure.

### An annual floor — the term that tests whether they're serious

**Ask for a guaranteed minimum of $25,000–50,000/year** in commission/revenue share regardless of how many customers they sell.

This costs them nothing if they actually intend to sell it. If they resist hard, that tells you they're not confident in the pipeline — which is the most useful thing you could learn in the whole negotiation.

## 4. The three definitions that decide whether any of this is real

Get these written down or the percentages are decoration.

**1. What counts as "nonprofit revenue"?**
Best: *"All revenue from any customer classified as a nonprofit, across all services and software lines."* Broad, simple, hard to argue with.
Worst: *"Revenue attributable to the SAGA module."* If SAGA is bundled into a $150k engagement with no allocation formula, this is zero.

**2. Bundle allocation.**
If they won't accept the broad definition, insist on **a fixed dollar figure per customer per month** — "$200 per nonprofit per month, regardless of what the bundle costs." Simple, verifiable, immune to creative accounting.

**3. Revenue, never profit.**
Profit is whatever's left after costs, and they decide what counts as a cost. Always revenue, always gross.

**Also ask for:** quarterly statements showing customer count and revenue, and the right to audit the books once a year (at your cost, at theirs if they're off by more than 5%).

## 5. Terms that are worth money even though they aren't money

Trade these deliberately — give one up to win a number you care about more.

| Term | Why it's worth real money |
|---|---|
| Title with "Lead" or "Owner" | Determines who gets pulled into every deal. Free to give, valuable to hold. |
| Named on every nonprofit engagement | Keeps you the go-to guy in practice, not just on paper. |
| Roadmap sign-off | Stops the product drifting away from what you know works. |
| Your name on the AppSource listing / case studies | Public credit builds *your* reputation, which is portable. |
| Certifications paid, in your name | You keep them if you leave. |
| **IP reverts if they stop selling it** | If it gets shelved, you get SAGA back. Costs them nothing to agree — they've stopped caring by then. |
| No non-compete in nonprofit software | If they insist, it's paid for and expires in ≤12 months. |
| Change-of-control clause | If Sync(d) is acquired, your deal survives or accelerates. |

## 6. Three packages you could actually put on the table

Bring options rather than one number. It signals flexibility and it moves the conversation from "yes/no" to "which one."

**Package 1 — Partnership (you own it)**
$500/mo list price · 30% reseller discount · 18-month Dynamics-only exclusivity against 12 customers/year · you own the publisher account and IP · named technical lead.
→ *~$50k/year to you, and you still own the company.*

**Package 2 — Employment plus upside (they own it)**
+$30k base · 7% of nonprofit engagement revenue · $40k IP payment · 2% equity, 4-year vest · $30k annual floor · Practice Lead title · IP reverts if discontinued.
→ *~$70–100k/year at ten customers, plus equity.*

**Package 3 — The middle (joint)**
They own the Dynamics/Dataverse version, you keep sagacrm.io and the SAGA brand outright · $150/customer/month for 5 years · Practice Lead title · no equity, no exclusivity on your standalone product.
→ *Clean, simple, and you keep an independent business.*

## 7. What to say in the room

You don't have to have all the answers. What you need is to not agree to a number on the spot.

> "I'm glad you're interested — I think nonprofits are a real market for Sync(d) and I want to be the one who builds it here. Before I can put a number on it, I need to understand what a nonprofit engagement actually bills out at. Can you walk me through what a typical Forge customer pays? Then let me come back with two or three ways we could structure this."

Then leave. Do not name a number in the first meeting. You are allowed to say *"let me think about it and come back to you"* to literally anything.

The one thing to say clearly and early, because it gets harder the longer you wait:

> "Before we go too deep — I want to get it in writing that SAGA is mine, since I built it on my own time. That protects both of us."

## 8. Reality check on your leverage

Be clear-eyed. Right now:

- **No confirmed paying customers.** Your production API reports one organization and one user.
- **The code is public under MIT**, so they can legally use it for free.
- **The four agents aren't built** — the selection logic runs, the sending doesn't.

That means arguments resting on "this is a valuable product" won't hold much weight. What you actually have leverage on:

1. **You're the only person there who understands nonprofit fundraising as an agent problem.** That is genuinely scarce and it's why they came to you.
2. **You have the design worked out** to build-guide depth — months of thinking they'd otherwise have to do.
3. **The brand, domain and live product** — not covered by MIT.
4. **Speed.** You get them into a new vertical faster than they'd get there alone.
5. **They approached you.** People don't do that from a position of indifference.

That combination supports a strong ask for **ongoing participation** — role, commission, a share of what gets built — and a weaker ask for **a big upfront IP payment**. Weight your asks accordingly, and the numbers above already do.

**And the cheapest thing that would raise every number here:** land two or three paying nonprofits first, even at $100/month. Two real customers changes the conversation from "an idea he built" to "a product with traction," and that's worth more than any negotiating tactic.
